Two Tablets, Ten Guests: The Thanksgiving Gomoku Arithmetic

2026-11-10 8 min read

Ten people arrive at 4:40, the plates clear, and the pie lands at 6:00. That is eighty minutes. One tablet on the coffee table gives each guest sixteen minutes of actual gomoku, because a board holds two players and there is no version of this game where it holds four. A second screen on the sideboard turns sixteen into thirty-two. Eighty times two times the number of boards, divided by the number of guests. The arithmetic is finished.

Most hosts add the second screen and expect the evening to double. It does not double. It halves the wait between games, which is worth having, and it is a different planning problem from the one people plan for.

A board holds two, and the script will not let you fake a third

There is no pass button and no way to skip a turn. Tap the board when it is not your turn and nothing happens; the status line still names the other colour. That rules out the two formats hosts reach for first: a team of two sharing one colour, and a rotation where the next person plays the next move. Both need the board to accept a stone out of turn. It will not.

So the unit of throughput is a pair. A pair spends about eight minutes on a 15×15 freestyle game that runs forty-odd moves, six on 9×9, which usually ends in the low twenties, and ten on 19×19. Multiply by the number of pairs that can sit at once and you have the evening. If there are six or more people and only one device, the one-device version of this problem is worth reading before the doorbell.

If you would rather run rounds than a queue, the bracket that fits between the plates and the pie uses the same eighty minutes and produces a name instead of a queue.

A second tab is not a second table

Two tabs on one laptop is one table. The board is drawn once per page, so only the visible tab holds a position anyone can look at, and the player waiting on the hidden tab is staring at a browser. Screens that can be watched at the same time are the only thing that counts.

A phone propped against the gravy boat counts. A tablet on the kitchen counter counts, if the kitchen is where people are standing. If the second screen is in another room, it is not a second table, it is a second game, and the two groups will not swap.

Nothing crosses between the two tables

No account, no saved game, no history. Close the tab and the evening is gone, and there is no list of what you played last year. Reload and the board comes back at 15×15, freestyle, against the computer on medium. Every table is set up on its own, every time, and a guest who reloads to fix a stuck tap has just put the AI back on the other side of the board.

The scoreboard will not help you compare the tables either. It counts by colour, not by name, and it never clears between games: black wins, white wins, draws, accumulated since the page loaded. Two tables means two counters both reading black 3, and neither of them knows who was black. Paper, one sheet per table, solves it. That is not nostalgia. It is the only place the two halves of the evening can be compared.

One of the two tables has to play freestyle

Renju needs a person within earshot. A forbidden move bounces off: the stone never lands, the position does not change, a message appears for two and a half seconds, and it is still black's turn. The message does not say which rule you broke. Double three, double four, overline, all three get the same two and a half seconds.

At the table where somebody knows the rules, that is enough, because the explanation arrives in one sentence. At the table where nobody does, three refusals in a row is a guest deciding the tablet is broken, or worse, that he is being cheated. Run freestyle on the unsupervised table. It is not a downgrade: overline wins, double threes are legal, and nobody has to adjudicate anything.

Do not hand a guest a tournament page

The ten championship pages on this site are pre-configured: 15×15, renju, against the computer, with the board-size and rule buttons disabled so the setting cannot drift. Good reading. Bad second table. A guest who lands on one can switch to vs Friend, but he cannot get to 9×9 and he cannot turn renju off, because those two rows are locked.

If the second table needs a small board, it needs the main page. Bookmark it, open it fresh, and set the three rows yourself.

Put the two slow players together

A position cannot be moved between devices. No save, no export, no way to hand a game from the tablet to the phone, so a game begun at one table ends at that table. That single limitation decides the pairings more than anything else does.

Sort by how long people think, not by age. Two players who both take thirty seconds a move will hold one table for fifteen minutes. Split them across the two tables and both tables stall at the same moment, which is worse than one slow table, because now four people are waiting instead of two. The fast pair belongs together too, at the table nearest the food, because they will finish first and want back in. When a table does start to stall, a per-move budget fixes it faster than a reminder: ten seconds a move changes which three moves are worth thinking about.

Five lines on the fridge

One sheet per table: date, board, rule, winner's name, and the move count if anyone kept it. By the end of the night you have two sheets and one number worth knowing, which table produced the shorter games. Expect the 9×9 sheet to run six to eight moves shorter than the 15×15 one, and expect the renju table to run longest, because a refused move costs a rethink.

Set both boards before the doorbell. Main page on each device, vs Friend on both, 9×9 freestyle on one and 15×15 on the other. Two minutes now against twenty minutes of argument at 5:15. If the second screen turns out to be a phone nobody wants to hold, drop to one table and run a single chair with a queue behind it instead.
