Gomoku Set Buying Guide: How to Choose a Board You Will Actually Play

2026-10-13 8 min read

A gomoku set is one of the cheapest serious games you can own, which is exactly why so many people end up with the wrong one. When the price is low the temptation is to buy the first decent-looking option, and the differences that actually matter are invisible in a thumbnail.

Work through these five decisions in order and you will skip most of the market.

Decision one: where will it live?

Answer this before anything else, because it eliminates more options than any other question. A board that never leaves the coffee table can be heavy, thick and beautiful. A board that goes in a rucksack twice a month has to fold. A board that gets used in a car has to be magnetic, or you will spend the journey retrieving stones from under the seats.

Most households need one board and most households are best served by a full-size wooden set. Be honest about whether you are that household. If a board is going to be stored in a cupboard, it will be played less than one that already sits open on a table.

Decision two: which grid size?

Grid size changes the game far more than stone quality ever will.

GridWhat it playsBuy it if
15×15Gomoku and renju — the standardYou want the real game. This is the default.
19×19Go, plus gomoku on the inner gridYou want one board for several games.
13×13A faster, shallower variantYou only ever want five-minute games.
9×9Teaching onlyNever, as your main board.

The rule is simple: 15×15 for gomoku, 19×19 if you also want Go, and nothing smaller than 13×13 for a board you intend to keep.

Decision three: can you actually see the lines?

This is where cheap boards fail, and it is almost impossible to judge from a product photo. Points you can check in the listing or in the reviews:

  • Are the lines described as printed, engraved or inlaid? Inlaid is best, engraved is fine, printing is the one that fades.
  • Are the star points (the small dots) marked at all? On a 15×15 board there should be five, as a reference grid for the centre. Their absence is a sign nobody involved in the product knew the game.
  • Do reviews mention the grid being faint, or lines filling with dust? That is a fatal flaw in a board, and people who notice it tend to say so loudly.

A board with a beautiful grain and an unreadable grid is worse than an ugly board you can play on.

Decision four: stones, count and feel

Stone material is the feature people agonise over and the one that matters least. Glass, ceramic, melamine and plastic all play identically; they differ in weight and in how they sound when you place them. Heavier stones feel better, and feeling better is a real reason to spend more, but it will not make you a stronger player.

What is not optional is count. You want roughly two hundred stones per colour. A gomoku game normally uses fewer than fifty per side, so a slim set will get you through a game — but the moment the count runs low you start counting stones instead of thinking about the board, and that is a bad habit to build.

Decision five: construction and storage

Look for a board that will not move under your hand, a hinge that sits flush if the board folds, and two bowls or trays. Bowls are the difference between a set and a pile. If two players are sharing one bag of stones, one of them will knock it over within a month.

Thickness is the last variable and the easiest to trade away when budget is tight. Thick boards sit flatter and sound better. Thin boards are perfectly playable. If you have to choose between thickness and a properly printed grid, choose the grid.

The short checklist

If you want to stop reading and buy, this is the whole guide compressed into one recommendation: a solid 15×15 board, a crisp grid with marked star points, two full sets of stones, two bowls. Everything else is a preference.

Five mistakes that waste money

Buying a 9×9 "travel" board as a main set. It is not the same game and you will outgrow it in a week.
Choosing stones before choosing a grid. Nobody ever lost a game because their stones were the wrong material.
Believing "magnetic" is a quality tier. It is a use case, not an upgrade — magnetic stones are smaller and the boards are usually thinner.
Buying a set with no bowls. See above. Every time.
Buying a board as a gift for someone who has never played. Teach them the rules first with the beginner guide and let them choose; a board chosen for them tends to sit unopened.

What a set should include

Board. Two full sets of stones. Two bowls. That is the complete list. Anything else in the box — a rule leaflet, a cloth bag, a score pad — is a bonus, and a rule leaflet is genuinely useful because house rules about overlines differ. But none of it compensates for a faint grid or half a set of stones.

If you are buying for a household

Buy once, buy the middle band, and buy the size everyone will play on. A cheaper board that gets used every week beats a beautiful one that gets used twice a year, and the glossary is a better investment than a thicker slab if there is anyone in the house still learning the vocabulary.

Frequently asked questions

Is an expensive board worth it?

Only if the cheap one has a bad grid. Price buys thickness, hardwood and stone feel; it does not buy a better game.

Should I buy a Go set and use it for gomoku?

Yes, if you want a 19×19 board for Go as well. Otherwise buy the 15×15, which is the board the game was designed around.

How do I know the stones will be enough?

Check the stated count, not the photo. Under about 150 per colour, keep looking.

The bottom line

Place, grid, lines, stones, construction — in that order. Almost every bad purchase is a decision made out of order: pretty stones and a faint grid, or a beautiful board that is two sizes too small for the game it is meant to play.
