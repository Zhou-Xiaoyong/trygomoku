Gomoku Live Three vs Dead Three: Which One Forces?

2026-10-07 8 min read

A Three Is Not Automatically a Threat

Beginners count stones; stronger players count options. The most useful single distinction in gomoku is the one between a live three and a dead three. Both are just three stones in a line. One of them forces your opponent to stop what they are doing and answer, right now. The other is decoration — and treating decoration as a threat is how players quietly hand away the initiative, one tempo at a time.

The difference is not in how the three looks. It is in what the three can still become.

The Only Test That Matters: One Move to an Open Four

Forget memorising shapes. Ask one question:

If I add one more stone here, do I get an open four?

An open four is four in a row with both ends free. It cannot be stopped: your opponent has one stone and two places to block, so they plug one end and you win at the other. An open four is therefore already a won game — which means anything that turns into an open four in a single move is a threat you are not allowed to ignore.

The two labels fall straight out of that test:

  • Live three (open three): one more stone turns it into an open four. It is a real threat, because it promises to win in two moves.
  • Dead three: no single stone turns it into anything forcing. It threatens nothing today.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this article is about applying the test quickly, and about the large grey area between the two extremes.

Live Three: The Shape That Forces

The textbook live three is three stones in a row with both ends empty and at least one square of breathing room beyond each end.

     c d e f g h
 8   . . . . . .
 9   . X X X . .
10   . . . . . .

The stones sit on d, e and f. Both empty squares work: play c and you hold c–f with b and g free; play g and you hold d–g with c and h free. Two different squares produce the same result — an open four. Your opponent cannot cover both, and that is precisely why a live three is forcing: it does not merely threaten, it collects a response.

This is the shape to internalise first. Nearly everything else in gomoku — forks, forcing sequences, endgame technique — is assembled out of live threes and live fours.

Dead Three: The Shape That Costs You Tempo

Now block both ends of that same three.

     c d e f g h
 9   . O X X X O .
10   . . . . . .

Three stones still, but c and g belong to your opponent. Hunt for a five: the only five-stone windows that contain d, e and f are b–f, c–g and d–h. The first needs c, the second needs both c and g, the third needs g. Every route is shut. This three can never become five along that line, no matter how long the game runs. It is genuinely dead — not "weak", not "sleeping", dead.

A dead three becomes actively harmful the moment you spend a move answering it: you have traded a tempo for nothing at all. The correct reply to a dead three is to play your own move and let your opponent waste theirs trying to revive a corpse.

Watch the board edge, because it catches people out. Three stones lined up along the edge have only one direction to grow in. One end is permanently the wall, so you can never build an open four off them — the edge quietly converts a shape that looks live into one that is merely sleeping.

The Middle Case: the Sleeping Three

Real games are rarely that tidy, and the binary "live or dead" hides the shape you will actually meet most often: a three with one end blocked and the other open.

     c d e f g h
 9   . O X X X . .
10   . . . . . .

Play g and you get d–g: a four with h open and c blocked. That is a rush four. It threatens five in exactly one square, so your opponent must answer it — but the block kills the line for good. The shape cannot win on its own. What it can do is buy you one forced move.

So a sleeping three is a stored tempo, not a threat. Whenever you happen to have a genuinely free move, spending it there manufactures a forcing four that costs your opponent a response. Calling that shape "just a dead three" throws away a resource; calling it a live three makes you panic for no reason. Both errors come from the same sloppy habit of sorting shapes into two boxes.

The practical rule has three tiers, not two: live three → answer it now. Sleeping three → note it and move on. Dead three → ignore it completely.

The Three Does Not Have to Be Solid

One more wrinkle, and it is the one that separates sharp readers from average ones: a three does not need three adjacent stones. A gapped three still counts as live if filling the hole makes an open four.

     c d e f g h
 9   . X X . X .
10   . . . . . .

Here the stones sit on d, e and g with a hole at f. Fill f and you hold d–g — an open four with c and h free. That is a live three, even though no two of its stones were ever touching.

Two consequences follow. When you scan the board for threats, scan for holes, not just solid runs; strong players spot the gapped live three long before the obvious one. And note the asymmetry: a contiguous live three gives you two squares to complete it, while a gapped one usually gives you exactly one. It still forces, but there is no slack — occupy that single square and the threat evaporates.

Live Threes Are the Raw Material of Forks

Why bother being precise about any of this? Because gomoku is almost always won by creating two threats at once, and live threes are what those threats are built from. A stone that makes a live three and a four, or two live threes crossing on a single point, creates a problem that no one move can solve. That construction is the double-threat fork, and it deserves one important caveat: the fork almost never appears by luck. It appears because you planted a live three two moves earlier and left it sitting on the board.

Conversely, sleeping and dead threes make poor fork material — they lack the second growth square that gives a threat its teeth. So the discipline of telling live from dead is not pedantry. It is the groundwork for the actual winning tactic.

Reading the Difference at Speed

Under time pressure you will not run the full test on every line. What you need is the reflex to classify a three on sight, which is a pattern-recognition skill rather than a calculation skill. Deliberate repetition builds it: take any game and, after every move, say out loud which threes exist and which of them are live. Pattern recognition in gomoku covers the drills in detail; the short version is that live-three detection should become automatic, so that your thinking time goes into counting moves instead of hunting for shapes.

Checking Yourself Against the AI

The computer is an honest examiner, because how it reacts to your shapes tells you whether you read them correctly:

  • It answers a live three. Immediately, every time. If you play one and the engine replies somewhere else, you misread the board.
  • It is happy to ignore a sleeping three. Not from carelessness, but because it has counted and found something faster. If it ignores yours, hunt for the counter-threat before you celebrate.
  • It treats a dead three as nothing at all. Use that both ways: a dead three of your own is a cheap probe of whether the engine is genuinely reading, and a dead three of its own is a square you can safely leave alone.

Then close the loop. Play a game on the board and, after each pair of moves, label every three — yours and your opponent's — as live, sleeping or dead. Players who keep that habit for a week stop repeating the same loss, because they finally see it happening in real time: answering a dead three, or letting a live one slide past unanswered. And with that reflex in place, the heavier machinery — forcing sequences, tempo counting, converting a lone four into a win — has something solid to stand on, which is exactly what advanced gomoku strategy is built on.

The 60-Second Checklist

  1. Find every three on the board — yours and theirs.
  2. For each one, ask the single question: can one stone make an open four?
  3. Yes — it is live, and if it belongs to your opponent you answer it now, no negotiating.
  4. One end blocked, or pinned against the edge — it is sleeping: a stored forcing move, not an emergency.
  5. Both ends blocked — it is dead: play your own game and let it rot.

Three stones is not a threat. Three stones that can still grow is a threat. Once you can tell those apart at a glance, you stop spending moves on shapes that cannot hurt you — and that recovered tempo is usually the whole game.
