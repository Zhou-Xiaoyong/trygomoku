Buying Guide

The Best Gomoku Board Sets for Home Play

2026-10-13 9 min read

Almost nobody sets out to buy a gomoku board and ends up with the right thing on the first try. The reason is simple: the 15×15 grid that gomoku uses is a standard board size that Go shops also sell, so the product you want is usually labelled something else. That is not bad news. It means there are far more decent boards on the market than the handful of products that happen to use the word "gomoku" in their title.

This guide is about picking one you will still be using in five years.

Why a physical board is worth owning

You can play the entire game in the browser, and for learning the rules that is genuinely the fastest route — no setup, no lost stones, no argument about whether a row is really five. A board adds three things a screen cannot.

First, the whole position is visible at once. On a screen you scroll, zoom and lose the edges; on a board your eye learns to sweep the grid in a single pass, which is most of what "reading the board" means. Second, your opponent is a person sitting opposite you, which changes the game socially far more than it changes it tactically. Third, and least obvious: a physical board makes losing cheap. You reset it in two seconds and play again, which is how beginners actually get better.

Almost every gomoku set is sold as a Go board

Gomoku needs a 15×15 grid. Go is played on 19×19. Renju, the competitive form of gomoku, uses 15×15 with strict opening rules. Because 15×15 boards are cheap to manufacture and Go boards are manufactured by the million, an enormous number of "gomoku" products are simply Go-family boards with a different label.

This matters when you shop, because it means the filtering you should do is not "does this say gomoku" but "is the grid the right size, and are the stones the right count". Everything else is decoration.

The four things that actually matter

Grid size. 15×15 is the standard and the one to buy. 19×19 also plays gomoku perfectly well — you simply ignore the outer rows — and it additionally plays Go. Smaller boards change the game into something else.

Whether you can see the lines. This is the single most common disappointment. On a cheap board the printed grid is faint, or it is cut so shallowly that it fills with dust, and after a month you are squinting at a blank slab. You want crisp, dark, evenly drawn lines with clear intersections.

Stone count. A full set is two baskets of roughly two hundred stones each. Anything under about 150 per colour means you will run out during a long game, and running out mid-game is exactly as annoying as it sounds.

Board thickness. Thicker boards are heavier, sit flatter, and make a satisfying click when a stone lands. Thickness is a comfort feature, not a rules feature, and it is the first thing that gets sacrificed to hit a lower price.

Wooden 15×15 sets: the safe default

If you are buying one board and you are not sure how much it will get used, buy a wooden 15×15 board with two full sets of stones. It is the shape the game is designed for, it looks good enough to leave out on a table, and it handles every variant of the rules without complaint.

Two things to check. First, that the board is a single piece rather than a thin veneer over particle board — a veneered board will bow if it ever gets damp. Second, that the stones come with a bowl or tray each, because loose stones in a plastic bag are how sets get lost one stone at a time.

This is also the set that works best if you intend to learn the competitive form later. When you move on to renju, the board is already correct; only the rules change.

Folding boards and travel sets

A folding board splits in half and the two halves become the lids of a box that holds the stones. It is the same grid, the same game, and it stores in roughly the footprint of a large hardback book. For anyone who does not have a permanent table, this is the practical choice.

The trade-off is flatness. A folding board that uses a cheap hinge will develop a ridge along the centre line, and a ridge in the middle of the grid is precisely where a lot of important gomoku moves happen. Look for a hinge that sits flush when the board is open.

Magnetic sets: cars, planes and classrooms

Magnetic boards are the answer to a specific problem: playing somewhere the stones would otherwise slide. A car on a motorway, a train table, a plane tray, a classroom desk, a picnic blanket on a slope. They are usually smaller than a full board, which also makes them fast to play.

Magnetic sets are not a replacement for a proper board — the stones are smaller and the feel is different — but they are the only type that survives being used in motion. Households with children in the back of a car tend to get more hours out of one of these than out of anything else they own.

19×19 Go boards that also play gomoku

If anyone in the house is curious about Go, or if you want one board that covers the widest range of two-player games, a 19×19 Go board is the flexible pick. Gomoku on a 19×19 grid is the same game with more room around the edges, which slightly dampens the value of an early rush to the centre and rewards a quieter build-up.

It is also, stone for stone, the best value per square centimetre of playable surface you can buy. The only real downside is that a full-size 19×19 board takes up more table.

What to avoid

Three categories are best left alone. Decorative mini-boards — the little 9×9 or 13×13 boards sold as ornaments or as "travel puzzles" — are not gomoku at all; the smaller grid turns a deep game into a fast one. Printed-card boards crease and warp within weeks. And sets with no stone bowls are the single most reliable predictor that the whole thing will be lost in pieces.

One more: be sceptical of boards photographed with the stones arranged in a pretty pattern rather than on intersections. It usually means the listing was written by someone who has not played.

What to expect to pay

Think in three bands rather than exact figures, because prices move constantly. At the budget end you get a thin board and adequate plastic stones — perfectly playable, and a fine way to find out whether the game sticks. Mid-range is where the good stuff lives: a solid wooden board, a cleanly printed grid, two full stone sets and bowls. Premium buys thickness, hardwood and stones that feel good in the hand; the game does not get better, the experience does.

Our honest recommendation for a first purchase is the middle band. It is the point at which the board stops being a toy you tolerate and becomes furniture you use.

FAQ

Is a 19×19 board too big for gomoku?

No. You play on the inner 15×15 and ignore the rest. It has a slightly different feel because the board edges are further away, but the rules are identical.

How many stones do I need?

Two sets of roughly 200 each. A gomoku game rarely uses more than 50 stones per side, but a full set means you never have to think about it, and it is also what Go needs.

Do I need a board at all if I play online?

Not to learn the rules. But the habit of sweeping the whole grid with your eyes — which is what most beginner mistakes come down to — develops noticeably faster on a board you can see all at once.

The bottom line

Buy a 15×15 wooden board with two full stone sets and a bowl for each colour. Add a magnetic set if you travel, a 19×19 Go board if you want one board for everything, and skip anything advertised primarily as a gift or an ornament. That is the whole decision.
