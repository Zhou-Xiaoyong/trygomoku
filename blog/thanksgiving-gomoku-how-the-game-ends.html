Where a Gomoku Game Ends: Five, Eighty-One, or Restart

2026-11-16 8 min read

Five past eight, crusts on the plates, and two people are arguing about a stone that is no longer on the board. One says that was five. The other says count again, that was six. Neither of them is looking at the screen.

The screen settled it four minutes ago, the moment the stone landed, and it will not be renegotiated however long the argument runs. Half the disputes at a holiday table are disputes the board has already closed.

The position is sealed before the banner appears

A completed five ends the game on the spot. The win is recorded, a line is drawn through the five stones, and the position stops accepting input. Taps are refused. Undo is refused as well, which is the part that surprises people: after that fifth stone there is no control on the page that takes it back.

The victory card shows up about a third of a second later, with a sound. In a loud dining room that gap does real work, because the sound is the announcement and the card is only the receipt.

So give the losing player the Restart button. The decision is already made and cannot be appealed. Handing over the tap turns the end of a game into a small courtesy instead of a negotiation.

Black's sixth stone never lands. White's sixth wins.

Under renju, black needs exactly five. A sixth stone in the same line is an overline, a forbidden shape, and this board refuses forbidden moves instead of penalizing them: the stone comes back off the grid, a warning sits there for two and a half seconds, and it is still black's turn.

White plays by a different rule. The check for white is five or more, in renju and freestyle both, so a white six wins outright.

Which produces the most confident wrong call of the evening. Someone counts six white stones in a row and announces that it does not count. It counts. The mirror case cannot happen, because black's sixth is rejected before it can become the sixth. If the table wants a rule both colors can argue about on even terms, that is freestyle, and the two rule sets differ in more than the fouls.

A draw needs eighty-one stones

No draw button, no offer, no handshake. The only draw this board recognizes is a grid with no empty point left: eighty-one stones on 9×9, two hundred and twenty-five on 15×15, three hundred and sixty-one on 19×19.

So when four people agree at move thirty that nobody is winning and start over, the counters record nothing. Not a draw, not two losses. The draw column sits at zero all evening, which is the tell that the table has been calling draws the machine never saw.

Two honest options. Play it out on 9×9, where 81 stones is reachable in a careful game, or write "called" on the paper and stop expecting the screen to agree.

Restart throws away the loss, not the counter

Restart clears the board and forgets the game. The three counters are left alone, and they only move when a game actually ends: a win adds one to that color, a full grid adds one to draws. An abandoned position is invisible to all of them.

That puts a bias into the evening's tally. Someone who quits twice while losing looks better on that scoreboard than someone who played both games out and lost both. "Black 4, White 2" counts finished games, not games played, and by ten o'clock those two numbers are rarely the same. The rest of the reset is a separate job, mostly about settings that survive it.

One more column on the paper

Four columns hold most evenings: name, color, result, and how it ended. The fourth is the one nobody writes and the only one that makes the first three honest, because it is where an abandoned game gets charged to somebody instead of evaporating.

Five words cover it. Five, six, full, called, quit. Four seconds per game.

  • Five is a normal win. Line drawn, counter moved.
  • Six is white only, and only in renju. Same counter, different argument.
  • Full means the grid ran out of points and the board called it.
  • Called means the table agreed and the machine never knew.
  • Quit means Restart mid-game. Nothing was recorded.

The ring marks the stone that lost it

The winning five keep a line through them, and the last stone played keeps its marker. So the answer to "how did I lose" is on screen, one line with a ring on the point that handed the game over. Two seconds of looking, then Restart.

That is not a review and the page has no history panel to build one from. But the ring usually sits on a stone that answered the wrong threat, and it is worth saying once, out loud, before the next game starts. Counting the line is the first thing beginners get wrong, and the ring makes it obvious without a lecture.

First move is the only handicap available, because stones cannot be spotted here and nobody can place twice. The move cap is a ceiling rather than a target: it exists so the person who is winning cannot turn one stone into a four-minute explanation while three people wait.

Four sentences, then play

Five ends the game and nothing takes it back. Black needs exactly five, white needs five or more. A draw means a full grid, and Restart means the game never happened. Write the fifth word on the paper yourself, because the screen will not.

Then hand the tablet to whoever lost and let them tap it. The board is a click away, the settings take ten seconds, and the next game is the only replay that matters.

Settle it on the board

Nine lines, freestyle for a mixed table, renju for two adults. Five ends it, nothing takes it back, and the paper gets the fifth word.
