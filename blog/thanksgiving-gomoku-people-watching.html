Thanksgiving Gomoku for the People Who Aren't Playing

2026-11-18 8 min read

Eleven moves into the first game somebody's aunt comes back from the kitchen with a dish towel and asks who is winning. Four people are in the room and not one of them answers in a single sentence. That is not a knowledge problem. The board holds exactly one piece of information about the past, and at that moment it is a small red dot on K7.

Everything below is about the people whose hands are not on the tablet: what they can actually read, what they will get wrong, and the one job that only they can do.

A finished game tells you who won and half of where

When a fifth stone lands the board stops taking input. The last stone will not come back off the grid and undo does nothing. What is left is two things drawn in a fixed order.

The winning line goes down first. Then every stone is redrawn on top of it. So the line survives only in the gaps between stones, and from the far side of a table it reads as a pale seam running through five pieces rather than a drawn line. Then the last-move dot is drawn last, over everything: a dot about a quarter of the width of a stone, bright red on black, a shade darker on white, with a thin pale ring around it.

That is the complete record of a Thanksgiving game. Five stones with a seam, and one dot. Who won, two seconds, from anywhere in the room. How, nothing at all.

The red dot is the entire memory of the game

There is no move list, no replay, no move numbers, no export. One marker, and it jumps. On 15×15 a game runs forty to forty-five moves, so a marker that moves every dozen seconds or so. Leave the room for forty seconds and you are four moves behind with no way back, because the position that existed when you left does not exist anywhere any more.

On 9×9 the same forty seconds is six moves out of twenty-two. Watching here is not a passive role. It is a real-time job or it is nothing.

Sound is the second channel, and it is thinner than it sounds

Every stone makes a 600 Hz sine tone lasting eighty milliseconds at low volume. Undo is a shorter, duller 300 Hz triangle. A win is four rising tones, C5, E5, G5 and C6, packed into a third of a second.

Eighty milliseconds at that volume disappears in a room with a dishwasher running and four conversations. The four-note rise does not. It is the only ending signal the room gets, and the only thing that tells the person at the sink that a game just finished and the tablet is free.

There is a sound button beside the board that switches between Sound and Muted. Somebody will mute it about eight moves in, because eighty milliseconds of clicking is more annoying than it is useful. Mute it after the last game instead. During play it is the only channel that reaches the people who are not looking.

Hands off the glass

In friend mode, any touch on the grid places a stone for whichever colour is on move. There is no out-of-turn guard in that mode; the guard that exists only runs against the computer. So pointing at a point with a finger is a move, and it is a move for the person whose turn it happens to be, not for the person who touched.

On 9×9 that is one wasted stone out of twenty-two, roughly five percent of a game, spent on a point nobody chose. On 15×15 it is survivable, but the player still has to explain a stone they did not pick while the other one works out what it was for.

So the seating is part of the setup. Tablet flat in the middle of the table. Two players on the long sides, facing each other. Spectators at the short end, or half a step behind a player's shoulder and outside arm's reach. Any arrangement where someone can sweep a hand over the grid mid-sentence is the wrong arrangement.

The one job only a spectator can do

Say the address of the move that just landed. Out loud. Two characters per move: H8, then I8, then H9. Column letter, row number, nothing else.

It has to be a spectator because the player's hand is on the glass and their eyes are on one line of it. The person behind the shoulder is the only one with the whole grid in view and empty hands. That is the entire division of labour, and it needs one person who can say a letter and a number without adding a comment.

Two rules keep it useful. Say the move that happened, not the move you think should happen next, because the second one is advice and advice from the side of the table wrecks a game faster than a bad move does. And say it before the next hand moves, because the dot has already left.

Twenty moves of this is a forty-character record of the game, which is more than any button on the page will give you. The same two-character habit turns up in playing a friend on a second device, where the reason is distance rather than a room full of relatives.

And here is the part that is not bookkeeping. A player looks at their own line. A spectator looks at four axes at once, and the shapes that decide these games sit on different axes. Black builds an open three along the e-row, white answers on the 7-file, and black's seventh stone quietly makes a second open three on the diagonal. Neither player sees both at the same moment, because each is still watching the line they just committed to. The person reading coordinates sees it and can say four words: you have two. That sentence is worth more than any advice about where to place a stone.

Only one player can be refused, which makes counting possible

Renju's foul check only runs for black. White is never refused a point. So a spectator who wants to help with the rules has one colour to watch and three shapes to learn: six or more in a row, two open threes at once, two fours at once.

The refusal is not a loss. The stone comes back off the grid, a warning appears in the middle of the screen and names the shape, and the turn stays with black. The warning clears in two and a half seconds, about as long as it takes the player to start hunting for a different point. The person standing behind is usually the only one who read it.

Three things a late arrival can still reconstruct

Somebody who walks in after a game has ended can take three facts off the board in about ten seconds. The seam through five stones: who won. The dot: where the fifth stone landed. The small colour dot beside the status line: whose turn it would be if the game were still running.

What cannot be reconstructed is the order. Not the number of moves, not the number of refusals, not the number of undos. Undo takes back one stone in friend mode and two against the computer, and neither leaves a mark, so a game with four undos looks exactly like a game with none.

The useless column is the last one, and it is the one that gets used. A hand over the grid costs a stone. A guess about who is ahead costs the room's confidence in the person who was actually counting.

If the room is bigger than the table

Six people and one tablet is a scheduling problem, not a viewing problem. Passing the tablet down a line costs about eight seconds of reading at every handoff, and the reading gets worse as the evening goes on.

Spectators are the argument for short games. A 9×9 game is twenty to twenty-four moves, which is twenty-four addresses out loud, spread across about six minutes. A minute of speech. A 19×19 game is sixty-five to seventy moves and nobody keeps that up past move forty. Shortening the game and keeping the room involved are the same decision.

One more thing worth saying before anyone sits down: the person reading coordinates is doing the job a club referee would do, and it is the only version of that job available here. A child can hold it, and often holds it better than an adult, because a child will say the address without attaching an opinion to it.

Two sentences before the first stone

Tablet in the middle, hands off the glass, one person saying the address of every move. If nobody in the room will do the reading, play on nine lines and keep the games short enough that losing track of one of them does not matter.

The board is one click away and the settings take a second: nine lines, Freestyle, vs Friend. The first five is about six minutes out.
