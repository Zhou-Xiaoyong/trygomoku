Looking for a Gomoku Analysis Tool? Review the Game With Three Buttons You Already Have

2026-11-23 8 min read

Stone 34 is where the game went wrong and you cannot prove it. The board shows a finished five, the score row says White 1, and there is no list of moves anywhere on the page.

This site has no analysis tool. No move list, no export, no per-move evaluation, no saved game. Refresh the page and the position is gone, along with the score. What it does have is three instruments that become a review kit once you stop expecting them to be an engine: undo, the hint button, and the depth counter that only appears on Hard and Master.

Below is how to use them to find the stone that lost, in about twelve minutes, without a single piece of software you do not already have open.

What the page will and will not tell you

Start with an honest inventory, because most review advice assumes a move list and you do not get one here.

  • It gives you: undo, which pops two stones against the computer and one against a friend. Hint, which marks the highest-scored point for whoever is to move, for three seconds. A score row that counts wins by colour across every game in the session. A depth readout while Hard or Master is thinking.
  • It does not give you: move numbers, a replay, an evaluation of the stone you actually played, or any way to reopen a finished game. undo returns immediately when the game is over, so the fifth stone in a row freezes the position permanently.

That last line is the one that changes how you review. The last moment you can inspect a lost position is before the winning stone is placed, not after.

Write the numbers down, or the game is gone

Against the computer you can skip this, because you can rebuild the opening from memory and the AI's replies are repeatable at the same tier. Against a friend you cannot, and a forty-stone game is not recoverable from a photograph of the final position.

The grid is fifteen lines each way. Number the columns a to o from the left and the rows 1 to 15 from the bottom. One stone becomes three characters: h8, h9, i9. Forty stones is a hundred and twenty characters, which fits on the back of a shopping list and takes about four seconds a move.

Two rules keep the record usable. One person writes and never plays, because the player always thinks he will remember. And if you are playing renju, mark the rejected stones: a forbidden double-three or overline bounces off the board with a two-second warning and Black keeps the turn, so the stone never enters the record. Skip that note and every move number after it is wrong by one.

The score row lies in a small way that matters here. It counts by colour, not by player, and it does not reset between games. Only a refresh clears it. Black 3, White 2 is a session total, not a match score, so do not use it to decide who was actually stronger.

Undo is a counterfactual, not a rewind

Against the computer, undo removes two stones: yours and the reply it drew. That is not a rewind to a tidy earlier position, it is the exact fork in the road. You get back the board as it stood before you chose, which is the only chance this page gives you to ask what the other move would have done.

Against a friend, undo removes one stone and hands the turn back to whoever placed it. That is a different tool. It lets you take back a stone in front of a witness, which is a social act, not an analytical one.

Used properly, undo answers one question per position: was the threat I was worried about actually the threat? Play the alternative stone, let the computer reply, and look at what it does. Then undo again and put your original stone back. The AI answers a different question each time, and the two replies together tell you whether your fear was real. What undo will not do is keep the branch: there is no second board, so the moment you re-place the original stone the experiment is gone. Write down the reply it gave you before you erase it.

Hint gives you the move, never the reason

The hint button takes the candidate list, sorts it by score, and marks the top point with a pulsing circle for three seconds. In a game against the computer it refuses to fire on the AI's turn. In a two-player game it works for whoever is to move, which makes it the only neutral third opinion available at the table.

As a review instrument it is a trap and a gift at once. Press it at the position where you froze, write down both points (the one you played and the one it marked) and then answer a single question in one sentence: what does its point threaten that yours did not? If you cannot name the threat, the hint taught you nothing. You collected an answer and skipped the method, which is exactly what a full analysis engine lets you do at scale.

Three seconds is also the right length for the test. Long enough to read the point, too short to talk yourself into agreeing with it.

The tier is the lens, the depth number is the receipt

Hard searches four plies (you, him, you, him) from twenty candidate moves. Any plan that needs your opponent to answer twice in a row dies at that depth, which makes Hard a clean test of whether your attack was forcing or merely hopeful. Master deepens from two plies toward twelve, up to forty candidates, and stops at a twelve-second deadline.

Replay the same opening at Hard, then at Master. The stone where their first choices disagree is the stone worth studying, because it sits exactly at the edge of what a four-ply search can see.

Master also prints a live Depth reading while it thinks, and that number is more informative than the move it finally plays. A position it finishes to depth ten or twelve in a second is narrow: a handful of forcing lines, few real branches. A position where it dies at depth four has too many live continuations to resolve in twelve seconds, and those are usually the positions where a human also has no idea what is going on. Note which of your games produced a shallow number; that is where your opening went soft.

Four questions, twelve minutes, one game

Review one game a week, not four, and review losses only. A win tells you what you already do well, which you can find out by playing. Set a twelve-minute timer and answer these four, in order, on paper.

  1. Where does the losing sequence start? Count backwards from his five: the four, the three that became the four, and the stone that made the three. The loss is almost never at the four. It is at the third stone from the end, the one that looked like development.
  2. How many tempos did you hand over? Count the moves between your last forcing move (a four, or a three he had to answer) and his four. More than two and you were playing alone while he was building.
  3. Which stone was quiet? One stone in his winning line was not a threat when he placed it. That is the one you had no reason to block and should have. Find it and you have found the pattern to drill, because it will come back in another game wearing different coordinates.
  4. Which move can you not explain? Not the worst move, the unexplainable one. If you cannot say in one sentence what a stone was for, that stone is the mistake, even if the game went on to be won.

If the timer runs out before you answer all four, stop. You were replaying, not reviewing, and replaying forty stones is just playing the game again with worse company.

What to do with the answer

One finding per game, converted into one drill for the next session. If the finding is a quiet third stone, play nine lines against Hard and stop every time your opponent gets three in a row without a four attached; that shape is the whole lesson. If the finding is tempo, take the practice routine and change only the question you write down before the first stone.

The reason to do this by hand rather than wait for a proper engine review is simple: the manual pass makes you name the threat out loud, and naming it is the part that transfers to the next game. An engine that shows you a number does the naming for you. For the broader picture of which habits actually move your results, this breakdown of what works is the place to start, and if your opponent of choice is the machine itself, the tier-by-tier guide to beating it covers what each one fails at.

Then close the notes app, open the board, and play one game you intend to review before you start it. Deciding to review afterwards is how the twelve minutes becomes zero.

Play a game you intend to review

Nine lines for shapes, fifteen for the real thing. Start the clock before the first stone, not after the loss.
