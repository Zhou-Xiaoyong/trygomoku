How to Block in Gomoku Effectively: A Defensive Playbook

2026-09-23 8 min read

Blocking Is Not the Passive Move

Most beginners treat blocking in gomoku as the move you make when there is nothing better to do. That framing is exactly why they lose. A well-timed block deletes your opponent's next idea, buys you a tempo, and often hands you the initiative. A late or badly-placed block just starts a chain of fires you can never quite put out. Learning to defend properly is worth more rating than learning one more fancy attacking pattern.

The aim of this guide is to turn blocking from a reflex into a decision: read the threat, rank it, place the stone where it actually cuts the line — and know when blocking is the wrong move altogether.

Rule 1: Count the Threats Before You Move

Almost every “how did I lose that” moment in gomoku comes from answering a threat that was never the real problem. Before you touch a stone, scan the board for three categories of danger:

  • An open four. Four in a row with both ends free, one move from five. If it is your turn and your opponent already has one, there is no defence — you can close only one end. Open fours have to be prevented one move earlier, always.
  • An open three. Three in a row with both ends free. Leave it and it becomes an open four, and an open four wins. This is the threat you must answer now.
  • A fork in progress. Any square that would create two of the above at once.

The rule of thumb: answer the open three, always; answer the closed three only when it is part of a bigger shape; never spend a move on a two that is doing nothing. Spotting these shapes fast is a separate skill — it is what pattern recognition in gomoku trains — and it is the single biggest lever on how fast you can defend.

Rule 2: Block the Intersection, Not the Nearest Stone

Where you place the blocking stone matters as much as remembering to place it. A horizontal open three can be stopped at two squares, but only one of them also helps your own vertical or diagonal line.

Block one end of an open three and the other end is still live: your opponent simply plays there, gets a closed four, and you have to answer all over again. That is a lost tempo, and sometimes it cannot be avoided. But when you do have a choice, prefer the square that:

  1. also cuts a diagonal or vertical line of your opponent's;
  2. connects to your own stones, so the block doubles as development;
  3. faces the centre, where your future lines have room to run.

This is the most profitable single upgrade for an intermediate player: the same defensive move, one square different, that also builds something. It is the bridge into advanced gomoku strategy — defence that quietly gains tempo instead of merely surviving.

Rule 3: Do Not Chase Every Threat

Over-blocking is the most expensive habit in the game, and it looks like solid play. You block one three, then the next, then the next — and then you lose to a threat that was built because you kept spending stones on the wrong side of the board.

Three signals that you are over-blocking:

  • All your stones are on the edges. Defensive blocks strand you away from the centre, where lines actually go somewhere.
  • You have blocked four moves in a row. If your opponent has produced a live threat on every single turn, the game was already lost several moves back. The fix is earlier prevention, not another block.
  • Your blocks do not connect. A block that links to nothing wastes a stone twice: it only postpones the threat and grows your own attack by zero.

If two or more of these are true, stop blocking and ask a different question: can I make a threat of my own? A counter-threat forces your opponent to answer you, which is a strictly better version of a block. The ways people burn moves like this are worth reading once, slowly: common gomoku mistakes.

Rule 4: Sometimes the Right Block Is a Threat

In a balanced position, the strongest defence is often an attack with a defensive side-effect. Say your opponent has an open three on one line and you have an open two on another. Extending your two into an open three does two things at once:

  1. it forces your opponent to stop and answer you;
  2. it keeps your own line growing instead of frozen.

It is a tempo exchange: you hand over the obligation to block. It only works if your threat is at least as urgent as theirs — an open three for an open three is fine, a sleepy two against an open three is suicide. When the threats are not comparable, block.

Rule 5: Know When Blocking Is Already Too Late

Two positions cannot be saved by a block, and recognising them early stops you from wasting moves that could have built a counter-attack:

  • Your opponent already has a double threat (a fork). Two independent lines, one stone. You can answer only one of them. If it is your move, go looking for your own fastest forcing sequence instead — a defensive move here is a resignation in slow motion.
  • Your opponent has an open four. The game ends next move whatever you do. A block buys one extra turn, and that only matters if that turn wins the game for you.

In both cases the move that saves you is almost never a block. It is the fastest forcing sequence you have, played immediately.

A Three-Step Blocking Checklist

Run this in the ten seconds before every defensive move. It is deliberately short, because long checklists never get used in a real game:

  1. What is the biggest threat right now? Open four > fork > open three > closed three.
  2. Can I answer it with a threat of my own? If yes, and the threats are comparable, play the threat.
  3. If I must block, is this the square that also builds? Same-line intersection, connected to my stones, facing the centre.

Three questions, in that order. Most club-level players who plateau are skipping question two.

Drilling It

Blocking is a reading skill, not a knowledge skill, so it has to be practised under pressure. A simple routine that works:

  • Play one game where you deliberately defend for the first ten moves, and after each move note which threat you answered and whether a threat of your own was available.
  • Replay the game afterwards and find the exact move where blocking stopped working.
  • Set the computer a notch above your comfort zone and try to reach move twenty without ever handing over an open four.

You can start that right now, free, with no download and no sign-up: open a board and play a game, giving yourself the rule “no defensive stone that does not also build.” It will feel awkward for ten minutes, and then something clicks.
