Thanksgiving Gomoku in Three Minutes: What Do You Say First?

2026-11-23 8 min read

It is 8:40 on Thanksgiving, the plates are stacked by the sink, and somebody asks whether there is a game. You have about three minutes of attention before the room splits into dishwashers and phone-scrollers. Tap versus friend first, because the board opens against the computer. Then nine lines. Then freestyle. Say five sentences while it settles. The tapping takes four seconds; the other two and a half minutes decide whether anyone plays a second game.

That order is not a matter of taste. It is what the buttons do to a game already in progress.

The board opens against the computer, so that is the first tap

Reload this page and you are on fifteen lines, freestyle, against the computer, medium difficulty. Nothing is stored between visits, so every session starts there. Hand the tablet over before changing the mode and your guest is not playing a person at all, and the mistake is invisible: on medium the machine answers in well under a second, so it feels like being answered, and the conversation about who won starts from the wrong premise.

It gets worse if a finger lands while the machine is thinking. In that mode a tap from anyone other than the player whose turn it is gets dropped without a sound. When the turn does belong to the human, the guest's tap spends your move. Their stone never appears, and the position quietly changes hands.

The third reason to do it first is blunt: every setting button on that panel ends the game in progress. Mode, board size, rule and colour all clear the board. So the order is mode, board, rule, and no stones until the last one. If somebody already started, you are no longer setting up. You are deciding whether to throw their game away.

Nine lines, and say the number out loud

Nine lines is twenty to twenty-four moves a game. Fifteen is forty to forty-five. Nineteen is sixty-five to seventy. That is the setting that decides how many games fit before the pie, and it changes the arithmetic by a factor of two.

Announce it anyway, because a guest who counts the lines and finds nine will assume you handed them the children's board. The answer is one sentence: fewer lines means every stone carries more weight, and a slow first four moves have nowhere to hide. On fifteen lines a beginner can drift for six moves and still recover. On nine they cannot, which is why the first game of the evening should be nine lines and the third, once everyone is warm, can be fifteen.

If the evening is already running late and a single game has to fit in a fixed window, that is a different problem with its own arithmetic, covered in the six-minute format.

Leave renju off, and explain the choice in one sentence

Renju forbids three shapes for black: a line of six or more, two fours at once, two open threes at once. This board does not penalise them, it refuses them. The stone bounces back to your finger, a warning holds for two and a half seconds, and black is asked to move again.

That is fair in a quiet room with two people who know the rule. At a holiday table it reads as a broken tablet, because the rejection arrives without anything the rejected player can act on. On the English page the warning is one sentence for all three shapes; the Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Russian pages name the shape that was refused. Either way it is gone in two and a half seconds, which is less time than it takes to read out loud over a running dishwasher.

So freestyle tonight. Renju is a second-evening project, and the gap between the two rulesets is worth reading properly before you switch it on in front of an audience.

If you want the difference settled before the holiday, the comparison of gomoku and renju covers what each one does to black's first ten moves.

The one sentence worth teaching

You have room for exactly one rule, so spend it on the three. A row of three stones with both ends open has to be answered now. The same three with one end blocked can wait a move, because it can only grow into a four in one place and you will see it coming.

The counting takes two seconds: take the stone just played, run each of the four lines through it, and ask how many stones sit in that line and whether the far ends are empty. A guest who learns only this loses the first game and understands why. That is a better outcome than losing five and learning nothing.

For a younger player the same rule needs shorter words, and the version written for children is the one to read off the screen rather than paraphrase.

Two buttons to declare off-limits before the first stone

Hint marks the highest-scoring empty point for whoever is on move, holds it for three seconds, and works in two-player mode as well. It is not advice, it is the answer, and one press ends the argument the game was about. Say it now, not after the first press.

Undo behaves differently in the two modes, and this is where a spoken rules announcement earns its keep. Against the computer it takes back your move and the reply together, two stones. Between two people it takes back exactly one stone and hands the turn back to whoever played it, so the person holding the tablet can delete their opponent's last move and nothing asks them to confirm. Either declare it once per game, or declare it gone for the night.

The five sentences, in the order you say them

Each one closes off a decision that would otherwise be made wrongly in the middle of the first game. At a normal pace the five take about fifty seconds.

  1. "Black starts, five in a row in any direction, diagonals included." That is the whole rule, plus the answer to the question that arrives on move three.
  2. "We are on nine lines tonight." Announced by you, not discovered by the guest counting.
  3. "Freestyle, so nothing is forbidden." Closes the renju question before somebody runs into the wall.
  4. "A three with both ends open has to be answered." The only piece of strategy worth spending breath on.
  5. "Undo is once each, and only for the stone you played." Exactly the argument that would otherwise happen at move eight.

A sixth sentence will not be heard. It is always a variant, a counter-example, or a story about a game somebody once won, and by then the first stone is already on the board.

That leaves ninety seconds for the question somebody always asks, which is whether diagonals count. They do, in all four directions, and the answer takes one demonstration rather than a paragraph. The row most people skip is the fourth one: a first game that starts without a spoken rule about undo will produce an undo argument inside five minutes, and that argument costs more than the setting did.

If the game is already wrong

Three situations, three different answers.

  • Three or four stones in, wrong mode. Clear it and say so out loud. Nobody remembers a four-stone position, and the announcement costs less than the silence that follows a quiet reset.
  • Twenty-five stones in on fifteen lines. Let them finish. A game that far along is the only thing happening at that table, and interrupting it to fix a setting teaches the room that settings outrank the game. Set up the next one correctly and out loud while the winner is still holding the tablet.
  • One guest against the computer, a second guest wants in. There is no way to add a player to that game, and the mode switch ends it. Decide out loud which of the two games is worth keeping, because the position cannot be saved and reloading will not bring it back.

And reloading is not an undo. It restores fifteen lines, freestyle, against the computer, medium, and zeroes the counter, so a refresh in the middle of an evening costs every setting you made plus the tally you were keeping.

If it is already too late

Ten-thirty, two people still awake, one game left in the evening. Nine lines, freestyle, one game, and ignore the scoreboard. That counter adds wins by colour, does not reset between games, and has no idea which person was behind which colour, so at that hour it reports the whole evening rather than the game in front of you. If the result matters, put it on the back of a napkin.

Then hand the tablet to whoever has been watching for the last hour without playing. A three-minute setup can still do that one useful thing. Everything else about putting a family evening together is scheduling, which is a longer and calmer subject.
