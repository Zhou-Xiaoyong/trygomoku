Gomoku Relay for Thanksgiving: Three to a Color

2026-11-14 8 min read

Move eleven, six people around one tablet, and the woman who just played White hands it to the wrong end of the table. Black plays. Then Black plays again, on a point nobody on White picked, and by the time anyone says wait the position has a hole in it.

Nobody cheated. The format just had no direction yet. A relay is the cheapest way to get six or eight people into one gomoku game on a holiday, and it needs one thing this board does not supply: a rule for where the tablet goes next.

A relay is one tap and a direction

The board tracks one thing, which color is on move. It alternates, one stone to a point, and it does not know how many people are sitting behind that color. Three players on White are the same White as one player on White.

So there is nothing to configure. One tap on vs Friend, one tap on 9×9, and the game is already a relay. No team mode exists, no pass exists, nothing hands a turn to a partner. What you add is a table convention: the tablet travels clockwise, every stone, all evening.

If you want the version where a pair argues over each stone before it lands, that is the two-on-a-side format, and it is a different game. Relay bets the other way. Nobody talks, everybody reads.

Every touch is a stone for whoever is on move

Against the computer, a stray tap while the AI is thinking does nothing. With humans, the only check in the code is that you cannot move for the AI in single-player. In two-player mode, any touch on an empty point is a legal stone for the color on move.

In a two-against-two game that is a nuisance: a partner's hand slips and your own side spends a move. With three to a color and a tablet making the rounds at a holiday table, the hand on the glass and the color on move are often not the same team. Someone reaches across for the salt and puts down a Black stone. You do not lose a move. You give one away.

Which makes the direction part of the rules, not the etiquette. The tablet leaves a hand only after the stone has landed and the ring has moved to it.

The foul gets charged to whoever is holding the tablet

Renju forbids three shapes for Black: six or more in a row, two fours at once, two threes at once. Here a forbidden point is refused, not punished. The stone bounces off, a message holds the screen for two and a half seconds, it never names which of the three rules you broke, and the turn is still Black's.

Now put three people on Black. The point that turns out to be forbidden is usually forbidden because of a stone a teammate placed four turns ago. The player who gets refused is third in the rotation, holding a tablet, being told nothing. Five relatives will decide that this particular cousin cannot count.

Two ways out. Run the relay on freestyle, which is the default here and the right call for any mixed table. Or keep renju and move the count to the handoff: the outgoing player counts the row out loud before passing, stones to the left, the new stone, stones to the right, and any total of six is refused. That is fifteen seconds of arithmetic on every move. Affordable at four players, absurd at eight.

The incoming player reads one ring

There is one marker on the board: the ring on the last stone. No move numbers, no threat highlights, no label telling you what the previous player intended when they chose that point.

That is enough if you narrow the job. Four lines run through the ring, and each gets two questions: how many stones are already on it, and is the near end open. Then one decision. Extend, or kill.

A row with three Black stones and one White at the far end is a three with one open side, and it can wait a move. The same three with empty points on both sides is a different object: one more stone turns it into a four with two winning points, and that is the shape nobody survives. You are not asking what he was thinking. You are counting how many points the line still threatens.

Say the answer out loud in one word, extend or kill, so the next person in the rotation inherits a plan instead of a puzzle. Eight seconds a handoff is enough for that. What you must not do is start a line of your own. Three stones is the whole game for you.

Nine lines, because every handoff costs eight seconds

Do the arithmetic before you pick a board. A 9×9 game runs twenty to twenty-four moves. Six players means three to a color, so three or four stones each, and twenty-odd handoffs. At eight seconds of reading apiece that is three minutes of the evening spent working out what the last person did.

On 15×15 those same six players get seven stones each across forty to forty-five moves. Double the overhead, and a second problem: seven stones is enough rope to start building something, which is exactly what a relay punishes. The strongest player will spend his seventh stone finishing a line he started at his second, and the other five will have been spectators for ten minutes.

The 19×19 board is for two people who have played for years and have the whole evening to themselves.

Handoff is seconds from receiving the tablet to placing a stone, and it is a cap rather than a target. The cap exists to stop a strong player from turning one stone into a five-minute explanation while five people wait for their turn.

Grandma, an eight-year-old, and one color

This is the line-up the format was built for, because relay is the only version of this game where the weakest player's move can be made almost free.

Handicap has no setting here. Stones cannot be spotted, there is no pass, nobody can place two. The lever is the order. Put the beginner first: the opening stone is the one move in the game that needs no reading at all, since all four lines through the center are empty and any of them can become the plan. Put the strongest player last, where the most information exists and the fewest moves remain to use it.

Then give the child one instruction instead of three: find the ring, look along the row, count the stones on each side of it. That is a real job, it takes ten seconds, and it is the whole skill the game asks for. The rules a child can hold stop at five in a row, blocking and fouls; relay only asks for the first two.

Settle one more thing before the first stone. Undo removes exactly one stone and returns the turn to whoever placed it, so in a relay the incoming player can delete the outgoing player's move. Rule that undo belongs to the person who placed the stone, and only while the tablet is still in their hand. Once it has been passed, the move stands.

Between games the list is shorter than people expect. The four buttons that end a game without asking all wipe the position, so whoever takes Black next checks the mode before anyone sits down. And if the queue behind the tablet gets long, the game-night rotation is the same arithmetic with more chairs.

Put the tablet at one end and pass it one way

Six people, 9×9, freestyle, eight seconds a handoff, strongest player last. The first game will take about eleven minutes and end with somebody surprised it is over. That is the format working, not a short game. Twenty-odd moves, six people, one hand on the glass at a time. The board is on the front page, and two-player mode is one tap right of the computer.
