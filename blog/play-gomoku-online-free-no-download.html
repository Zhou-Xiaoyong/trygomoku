First Stone, No Installer

Hand the phone to someone who has never played and the board is up before they finish asking what the rules are. Tap the centre intersection, the AI answers, and a game is running. No store page, no sign-in, no fifteen-second ad standing between you and the first stone.

That is the whole promise of playing in a browser, and it is smaller than it sounds. One game, maybe two. The reason it matters is not disk space.

An install costs nothing in megabytes and a great deal in commitment. The download turns "I have ten minutes" into "I am a person who plays gomoku", and most people who wanted one game at lunch stop right there.

What "No Download" Actually Changes

Three things, and only one of them is technical.

  • A board on whatever screen is in front of you. A work laptop, a borrowed tablet, the phone in your pocket. Same board, same four levels, nothing to sync because nothing is stored on the device.
  • No account, so no profile to keep. Nothing records your win rate. That is a relief if you play to think and a real loss if you play to measure yourself.
  • The position lives in the URL. Bookmark the page after move thirty, open that bookmark on another machine tonight, and the stones are where you left them. An installed app cannot do that.

The full set of controls, from the Renju toggle to the hint overlay, is on the features page. None of it needs configuring before you play, which is the point.

The First Three Minutes

Five in a row, any direction, and whoever completes it first wins. That is the whole rule set a first game needs. The longer explanation covers the variants, and they matter more than beginners expect.

Then three decisions, and stones.

  1. Board size. Leave it at 15×15. That is the standard grid, and every pattern you will read about assumes it. Nine by nine is for teaching children; 19×19 belongs to a different game.
  2. Opponent. Easy mixes randomness into a shallow score, so it will miss a live three and block something unrelated. Medium looks a couple of moves ahead and punishes a four you leave hanging. Take Medium if you know the rules, Easy if you are teaching somebody. The level-by-level guide covers what changes above Medium.
  3. First stone. H8, the centre. From the middle, four directions give you four points of runway each. From the corner you get two, and both are short.

That is the whole setup. Everything after it is the game.

Rules Travel, Settings Do Not

Two sites can both say gomoku and disagree about the most important point on the board. Freestyle counts six or more in a row as a win. Standard rules usually do not. Renju forbids the overline to Black outright, along with double-threes and double-fours, and a forbidden move loses the game on the spot in tournament play.

So check the rule set before the first stone. If your last game was on a freestyle app and this board has Renju switched on, the triple-three you are about to build loses immediately, and no amount of reading ahead warns you, because the rules are the problem and not the position.

The Tap Problem on a Phone

Fifteen lines across the width of the board. On a 360-pixel phone screen the gap between intersections lands around 23 pixels, and a fingertip covers closer to 45. Two adjacent points sit inside one finger.

On this grid a mis-tap is not cosmetic. Put a stone one point off a live three and you have two disconnected twos, no threat, and a move the opponent answers at leisure. In Renju the same slip costs more: a sixth stone in a row is an overline, and an overline loses for Black immediately.

Two habits cover most of it. Zoom in one step before touching a crowded cluster, and tap the point instead of dragging toward it. If that area is already dense, turn the phone sideways first and you get the width back.

Free, Specifically

Free here means three things you can verify in ten seconds: no paywall on the stronger levels, no registration before the first stone, and no ad between your move and the reply. The page carries display advertising around the board, which is how a site with no subscription stays online.

Worth checking, because "free online gomoku" covers a lot of ground. Some sites lock the good opponent behind a login, some stop you after five games, some put an interstitial in front of every rematch. All three are free in the sense that no money changes hands.

What You Give Up

No rating, no game history, no engine to show you where the loss started. Those are the real costs of skipping the account, and they only bite once you care about improving.

Two workarounds that cost nothing. Keep a text file with the date, the result, and the move number where the game turned. And after a loss you want to study, copy the URL before closing the tab, because the position survives exactly as long as the address you saved.

Notation runs two characters per move: H8, I8, H9. A forty-move game takes under a minute to write out by hand, and a week later that sheet is worth more than any win-rate number.

When an App Is the Right Call

Three cases. You are on a plane with no connection. You are playing a timed tournament game and need a clock you trust. You want an engine to refute your own move afterwards.

Everything else, the browser is the better tool, and not because it plays stronger. Because you will actually open it.

Ten Moves, Then Count

Open the board, leave it at 15×15, take Medium, stone one at H8. Play ten moves without undoing anything, then count how many of your stones sit on a line you still intend to use. Six out of ten is a normal opening. Four means the attack is already decoration, and ten moves are cheap enough to replay from the start.
