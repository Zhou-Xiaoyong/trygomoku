The Eight-Year-Old Runs the Table

2026-11-09 8 min read

At 4:40 the tablet changes hands and the eight-year-old does the thing that eats two minutes: he starts playing. The screen reads vs Computer, medium, and the first stone he taps gets an answer before he has let go of the idea that he is a player. He is not. He is the referee, and the referee's first job is not the game. It is the setting.

Three jobs the board already took

A referee normally does four things: keeps time, keeps turn order, calls fouls, declares the result. This board does three of them unasked.

Turn order is not negotiable. You cannot put a White stone down on Black's turn. The tap does nothing, and the status line still says Black.

The result is not a judgment. The fifth stone lights the line and the game is over. Nobody has to agree on whether it counted, because nobody is counting.

Fouls are refused rather than punished. In renju mode a forbidden Black move bounces off: the stone never lands, the position is untouched, a message sits on screen for two and a half seconds, and it is still Black's turn. A refused move is not a lost game. That single design choice is why a nine-year-old can referee without costing anyone a win.

What is left over is scheduling. That is a real job, and it is the right size for a child.

The one question before anyone sits down

Nothing on this board is remembered between visits. No saved game, no name, no streak, and the mode comes back up at vs Computer with the difficulty on medium. Hand a fresh tablet to a guest and the first move gets an answer within twelve seconds.

So the referee's opening line is not "who wants to play." It is: is the mode on vs Friend?

One tap settles it. In vs Friend the difficulty row and the colour row grey out, because neither means anything once both colours belong to the people in the room. Grey rows are the fastest proof the tablet is ready, and a child can check that in half a second without reading a word.

Board size is the second check, and it should be chosen before the queue forms rather than during an argument. 9×9 for a line that includes kids, 15×15 for two adults, 19×19 only for people who have already played twice tonight and want a long one. If there are six or more people and one device, the family game night setup covers that arithmetic.

Counting stones, which the adults will get wrong

Even number on the board, Black to move. Odd number, White. That is the whole rule, and it settles the two arguments that actually break out at a holiday table: whose turn it is after somebody took a call, and whether the position on screen is the one you stopped at.

It has a trap. Undo in vs Friend takes back one stone. Two taps take back two, and the parity flips with them. The referee counts before handing the tablet back, every time.

The one row a child will miscall

If the table is playing renju, teach the referee exactly one sentence: count Black's row, do not count White's.

Black needs five, exactly five. Six is refused by the board. White needs five or more, so White's six in a row is an ordinary win. A child who has been told "five in a row wins" will look at a White six and either call it wrong or, worse, hesitate, and the hesitation is what turns a finished game into a five-minute debate with an uncle.

Five minutes on the rules the night before is worth it. The kid-level version covers the same ground without the tournament vocabulary.

The envelope is the only record

This board keeps no history. Close the tab and the games are gone. There is no export, no account, no list of what you played in 2024.

So the referee holds the pen. Date, board size, rule, winner: one line per game, on the back of an envelope. A child will fill it in without being asked if you hand over an actual pen instead of suggesting a note on a phone. By Friday you have a scoresheet instead of a memory, and the scoresheet tells you something the evening did not: which board produced the shortest games. Expect 9×9 to run six to eight moves shorter than 15×15. Write it down and check.

Then the referee sits down

At some point the queue empties and the referee gets a game. Give it to him on a 9×9 board and let him take Black.

First-move advantage in gomoku is large. On 15×15 Black's edge is roughly a stone and a half; on 9×9 the same edge weighs more, because the board runs out of room for White to build a second threat. A 9×9 board plus Black is a handicap a child can hold in his head. "You get two stones on the rim" is not: it needs a placement he will fumble twice, and a fumbled handicap is a fight.

The second lever, if the adult is much stronger, is not stones but the undo button. One per game, for the child only. Not per move. A single take-back he spends when he wants, which teaches him to notice the moment he needs it.

Hand it over before the pie

Say one sentence when you pass the tablet: you are not playing, you are running it. Then watch whether the games get shorter. They do, because a referee who is waiting for his own turn keeps the queue moving instead of letting one game sprawl.

If the crowd is bigger than one table can hold, run a bracket instead of a queue. The 4:40 to 6:00 version fits between the plates and the pie, and it needs the same referee. If two people want to keep playing after everyone else has gone home, the browser tab is not the only option: playing a friend online survives the drive home.
