Nobody Starts the Thanksgiving Gomoku Game. Play Eight Moves Alone.

2026-11-26 8 min read

Four-forty on Thursday. The plates are stacked at one end of the table, the coffee is going round, and someone has said "we should play something" twice. Nobody has moved.

What is stuck is not the board. It is the absence of one person willing to start alone. Open the board, tap versus computer, tap white, and play eight moves with the room watching. Then switch to versus friend, which clears the demo and hands the game over in a single tap. Ninety seconds, no speech.

Why the table stalls

Three people want to play, one wants to watch, and all four are waiting to hear the rules before they commit. Nobody wants to be the one who loses first in front of relatives they see twice a year, so the conversation stays on the safe side of the first stone and the evening closes with the board shut.

A better explanation will not fix that. A game already in progress will.

Pick white and let the machine place the first stone

Versus computer is the page's default mode, and in that mode the colour row is live. Tap white. The machine now opens, and its first stone always lands on the exact centre intersection: the seventh line on a fifteen-line board, the fifth on a nine-line board. It checks that point before it scores anything else, so this is the order of operations rather than a preference.

One stone there does more than a paragraph about the middle. Someone will ask why it went there, and you can point at it instead of answering. Five or six more stones and the room has watched a legal game with a stone landing every few seconds, and nobody has been taught anything.

Eight moves is enough. Long enough for the shape to be visible, short enough that nobody has started narrating. Medium is the right opponent for this: easy blunders too early to look like a game, hard spends up to twelve seconds on a reply and turns the demo into a wait. The four tiers behave quite differently.

Let the hint button answer the questions

Two moves in, someone asks why you blocked a stone that was not yet a threat. Do not explain. Tap hint.

Hint scores every empty point for the side to move, marks the best one, and pulses it for three seconds before forgetting it. It works in versus friend as well as against the machine, and it always scores for whoever is on move, so the table sees the same answer the player would get. That is the difference between a two-minute argument and a three-second demonstration.

It is locked while the machine is thinking and after the game ends, so press it on your own turn. Then settle one thing before the first real game: whether hint is allowed. On nine lines the highest-scored point is usually the winning point, so a hint there is not advice. It is the answer.

The handover clears the demo by itself

Switching to versus friend resets the board. Every settings button on the page does the same thing, which is irritating at move thirty and convenient at four-forty-one: the demo disappears, the score row survives, and the difficulty and colour rows grey out because two humans need neither.

So hand the tablet over mid-sentence. No "let me clear it first", no debate about whether the demo counted.

Who goes first, decided once

In this mode the colour row is disabled and every reset opens with black to move. The first move is therefore not a setting and not a negotiation. It belongs to whoever is holding the tablet.

Give it to the person who asked the first question. They are already interested and will not freeze, and moving first is the only handicap this board can pay without a referee.

One rule out loud, not five

Say this before black plays: a three with both ends open has to be answered now, and everything else can wait a move. That sentence decides most family games on nine lines, where the board is dense enough for a live three to appear around move six.

Leave renju for later, if at all. Under renju black must make exactly five, a sixth stone is refused and bounces back with a two-and-a-half second warning while black keeps the turn, and white wins on five or more. The asymmetry is fair and it needs a referee. At four-forty it needs a referee and a lawyer. Freestyle, where either colour wins on five or more, is the right rule for a cold start.

The page runs no clock, so those seconds are what you agree out loud, and in a loud room they hold about as well as any house rule. Handicap has the same status: no handicap button, no komi, no pass. The levers are board size, who takes black, and dead stones parked on the outer line, two on nine lines and three on fifteen or nineteen, never touching, because a connected group of dead stones is just a stone.

Why the first real game is on nine lines

Not because it is easy. Because it is short, and because on nine lines the middle matters from the first stone. A nine-point line offers five places to start a five, and all five cross the centre point. On fifteen lines a line has eleven starts and exactly one crosses the middle. The centre is a slow positional question at fifteen and the entire question at nine.

That is also what makes a twenty-four-stone game feel like a finished argument instead of an opening. Two people who have never played can settle one while the coffee is still hot, and the loser usually knows which move cost them the game. Knowing that is what produces a second game.

The eight-year-old will reach first

Give the child black. Nine lines with the first move is a real advantage, and there is one sentence to learn: a three open at both ends has to be answered. Skip the rest of it.

Then watch the score row. It counts wins by colour rather than by person, and it does not clear between games, so a child who checks it after three rounds will treat it as a ranking. If that matters tonight, keep the tally on paper, where a name can sit next to the number.

If it is already past eight

Skip the bracket. Four rounds on nine lines is forty minutes of arithmetic and somebody leaves before the final. Play one game with a twenty-move cap, count it, stop. The six-minute format exists for exactly that exit.

If the room has shrunk to two people who both know the rules, put renju on and use fifteen lines. That is a different evening, and the first sixty seconds of it are already written down.

No tablet, or no signal: the board is a static page, so it works after one load, and the paper version needs one pen.

Open the board before the plates are cleared. White, eight moves, hand it over. Nine lines for the first real game, one rule spoken out loud, and a move cap agreed before the first stone.
