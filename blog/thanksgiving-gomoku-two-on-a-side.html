Two on a Side: Thanksgiving Gomoku for Four

2026-11-13 8 min read

Move nine and both people on White are reaching for the tablet. One wants G7 to cut the diagonal, the other wants J6 to start a line of their own. By the time they settle it, Black has played F8 and the argument is about a point that no longer exists.

Two against two fits a holiday table better than a bracket, and it is the only format where the argument happens before the stone instead of after it. It also has three failure modes nobody mentions in advance, all of them built into the way this board works.

Four people, two colors, no team button

There is no team mode, no pass, and no way to hand a turn to a partner. That sounds like a reason not to try it. It is the opposite.

The board tracks one thing: which color is on move. It alternates, one stone to a point, and it does not care how many humans are sitting behind that color. Put two people on White and the board sees the same White it sees in a one-on-one game. Nothing to configure past one tap on vs Friend, and nothing that breaks. If you have only ever played one against one on a single device, the pass-and-play basics take two minutes and cover the rest.

A rengo game of Go needs extra rules for two players a side, because captures and ko make a shared turn ambiguous. Here the shared turn is the least ambiguous thing on the table. Black, White, Black. The pair talks in the gaps the board already leaves.

The pair that taps first is Black

In two-player mode the color row goes gray and stops responding. Every game opens with Black, every reset puts the turn back on Black, and no button on the page moves it. So the assignment is not a setting, it is an action: whoever places the first stone is Black.

Use that. Hand the tablet to the weaker pair and let them open. On 15×15 the first stone is worth more than any junk stone you could give away, and it costs nothing to explain. Then say the second half out loud, because it is the part people forget: game two also starts with Black. If the tablet does not change hands, the same pair is White all evening.

One hand on the glass

Against the computer, a tap while the AI is thinking does nothing at all. With two humans, every touch is a legal move for whichever color is on turn. Four people around one screen means the partner's stray tap lands on your own color, which is worse than it sounds. The stone is not stolen from the opponent, it is spent by your own side on a point nobody chose.

On 15×15 an opening tempo is a nuisance you can play around. On 9×9, where a whole game runs twenty-odd moves, one wasted stone is usually the game. So the person on move holds the tablet, the partner's hands stay off the glass, and the partner speaks instead.

Undo removes exactly one stone and gives the turn back to whoever played it. Inside a pair that cuts both ways: it repairs a stray tap in one press, and it also lets your own partner delete the move you just made. Settle it before the first stone. Undo belongs to whoever is holding the tablet, and to nobody else.

A pair plays the move it can explain

This is the real cost of consulting, and it has nothing to do with speed. A block can be justified in four words. A double threat cannot: it needs two lines, two follow-ups, and a sentence that survives being interrupted.

So pairs drift toward the explainable move. Watch one consulting game and count how often a side plays the fork it saw against how often it plays the block it could describe. The gap is the format's tax.

One rule pays it back. Before the tap, the holder names what the stone does in one sentence, and the partner gets one sentence back. If the holder cannot name it, the move is probably a threat rather than a block, and it should still be played, with the words "I will explain in three moves" attached. Pairs that only play what they can name lose to pairs that allow one unexplainable stone in five. A double threat is the move that resists explanation, which is exactly why it works on a table full of talkers.

White's half of the conversation is one question

Black owns the initiative. The black pair talks between moves with a plan already in hand. The white pair answers, answers are always a tempo late, and so white has no planning time to spend on debate.

Give White a single question: which of their threes is still live? A three blocked at one end can wait a move. A three open at both ends cannot, because the next stone turns it into a four with two winning points. Checking costs two seconds per three, and there are never more than three worth checking.

If the white pair spends twenty seconds comparing two blocks, black plays the follow-up and the comparison was academic. Name the threat, then take the point that both blocks and builds. That is the whole consultation on the defensive side.

Two readers make renju survivable

Renju forbids Black three shapes: six or more in a row, two fours at once, two threes at once. This board refuses them rather than punishing them. The stone bounces, the position stays put, a message holds the screen for two and a half seconds, it never says which rule was broken, and the turn is still Black's.

One beginner plus a silent toast is a guest deciding the tablet is faulty. Two players on the same color is the only setup where the foul gets caught before the tap, because the partner counts the line while the holder is still choosing a point.

The count is mechanical. Extending a row means counting stones to the left of the new point, stones to the right of it, and the new stone itself. Four on one side plus the stone plus one on the other is six, and six is refused. Do the two diagonals as well and the double-three question answers itself, since a point that makes two open threes has to be open in two directions at once.

Choose the rule set before the first stone. Switching freestyle to renju clears the board, so a mid-evening conversion costs a position and an argument. The order a child can hold these rules in is five in a row, then blocks, then fouls, and in a pair the second player is the one who keeps the third item honest.

Per move means seconds to agree, not seconds to think. Assume twenty-odd moves on 9×9 and forty-odd on 15×15. Twenty seconds of consultation per move turns a 9×9 game into fifteen minutes, which is why four players belong on the small board and not on the big one.

Balancing four players with no handicap setting

There is no handicap setting here. Stones cannot be spotted before the game, there is no pass, and no way to place two and hand over. The only stone a stronger player can give away is a move spent on a dead point at the rim: first or last row, first or last file, then kept out of every line he needs. On 9×9 that works twice. Three is not a handicap, it is a resignation.

In a pair, announce the junk stone out loud. An unannounced rim stone reads to your partner as a real move, and the partner will build on it for two moves before working out that the line was never going anywhere.

The cheaper lever is the pairing itself. Rank the four players and put the strongest with the weakest against the two in the middle: 1 with 4, 2 with 3. The sums match, and the ringer cannot carry, because the player he has to carry is on his own side of the board. Then give Black to the pair whose weaker member is weaker, since Black forgives a slow start and White does not.

Ten seconds each, then the holder taps

Two sentences per move, one from each partner, then a stone. Twenty-four moves on 9×9 works out to eight minutes of play and four minutes of talk. Without the cap it is twelve minutes of talk and a queue forming behind you.

If a pair talks through three moves in a row with no stone landing, the problem is not the position. Neither of them has named the threat yet. Name it, tap, and let the next move be the argument. The board is on the front page, two-player mode is one tap right of the computer, and the first stone belongs to whoever is holding the tablet. With more than four in the room, the format changes rather than the rules: the game-night rotation covers what to do with the two people waiting.

Put two people on each color

One tap to two-player mode, the tablet in front of the weaker pair, and the first stone is theirs. Nothing else on the page needs setting.
