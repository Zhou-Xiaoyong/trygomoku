Gomoku Rules for Kids Explained: A Simple Guide for Parents and Teachers

2026-07-29 7 min read

If you want gomoku rules for kids explained in one sentence, here it is: take turns placing stones, and the first player to line up five in a row wins. That is genuinely the whole game. There are no captures, no special pieces, no scoring to memorize — which is exactly why gomoku works so well for children as young as five or six.

This guide gives you kid-friendly wording for each rule, a 10-minute teaching plan, and a few house rules that make the game fairer and more fun for young players.

The Rules, in Words a Child Understands

Skip the formal rulebook. Try phrases like these instead:

  • The board: "See all these crossing lines? We put our stones where the lines meet — on the little crosses, not inside the squares."
  • The stones: "You are black, I am white. Black goes first. Once a stone is down, it stays there forever — no moving it later."
  • Taking turns: "One stone for you, one stone for me. Nobody skips a turn, and nobody plays twice."
  • Winning: "Make a train of five stones of your color — straight across, straight down, or diagonal like a slide. Five in a row and you shout 'Gomoku!'"

That last image matters. Kids remember "build a train of five" far better than "form an unbroken line of five stones orthogonally or diagonally."

A 10-Minute Teaching Plan

  1. Minutes 1–2: Show, don't tell. Place five stones in a row yourself and say "this is how you win." Then show a diagonal five. Let your child build one too.
  2. Minutes 3–5: Play an open-hands game. Play slowly with all hints allowed. Point at your own threats: "Look, I have three in a row — what happens if you don't stop me?"
  3. Minutes 6–10: Play a real game. Let them win the first one if you can do it subtly. A first-game win is rocket fuel for a new player.

The single most important concept to teach after the basic rule is blocking: when the other player has three in a row with open ends, you must respond. Turn it into a spotting game — "alarm bells!" — and children pick it up within a few sessions. Kid-Friendly House Rules

Standard gomoku is played on a 15×15 board, but nothing stops you from adapting. These tweaks work well for younger children:

  • Shrink the board. For ages 5–7, mark off a 9×9 or 11×11 area. Games end faster and the child's attention lasts the whole game.
  • Play best-of-three. Short matches mean a lost game is never the end of the world — there is always the next round.
  • Allow one "take-back" per game. Formally this is heresy; pedagogically it is gold. One undo teaches kids to notice their own mistakes.
  • Start near the center. Ask them to place the first stone in the middle area. It is a genuinely stronger move, and it plants an early seed of strategy — the same idea behind the classic gomoku openings they can explore when they are older.

Winning, Losing, and Keeping It Fun

Gomoku games are short — usually five to fifteen minutes — which is a hidden advantage: a child who loses is only ever a few minutes away from a fresh start. Still, a few habits help:

  • Celebrate good moves, not just wins: "That block was brilliant — you saw my plan!"
  • Narrate your own thinking out loud sometimes. Kids learn to plan by hearing planning.
  • Do not always let them win. Children can tell, and a genuine hard-won victory is worth ten gifted ones.
