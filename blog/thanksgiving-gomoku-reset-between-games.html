Game Four Is Where Thanksgiving Gomoku Breaks

2026-11-12 8 min read

Game four starts at 7:10 and nobody can say what happened to game three. Someone tapped vs Computer to show a cousin how the engine answers a double three, the position went blank, and the board came back with a robot sitting opposite a thirteen-year-old. By 7:12 the argument is settled and the game is not.

Nothing broke. The board did what it was built to do. What it never promised was to hold the state of the evening: whose turn it is in the queue, whether this round is renju, who was ahead, and which of the six people in the room is black.

Three promises the board keeps, one it never made

It enforces alternation, one stone to a point, and it counts five in a row along all four axes. In renju it also refuses an illegal black move: the stone bounces, the position stays put, a message holds the screen for two and a half seconds, and the turn is still black’s.

That is a complete referee for one game. It is not a record of an evening. There is no account, no saved position, no session, nothing written to the device. Reload the page and the board returns at 15×15, freestyle, against the computer, on medium, with the scoreboard at zero.

That last sentence is the whole problem. A reload does not clear the stones and leave the settings alone. It rewrites the rules, the board size, the mode and the score in about a second, and it looks like a repair while it does it. Guests reload when a tap seems stuck. They are right about the tap and wrong about everything else.

Four buttons that end a game, and not one of them asks

The mode row is the expensive one. Tapping vs Computer calls the reset before anything else can happen: no confirmation, no road back to the position. It also leaves the board in single-player, so returning to two humans needs a second tap on vs Friend, which resets again. One demonstration, two dead positions.

The rule row does the same. Switching freestyle to renju and back clears the board, every board size button clears it, and so does the colour row, which is dead in two-player mode anyway. None of these are hidden. They are the buttons people press while they are talking.

One detail turns the wipe into an instrument. Each of those buttons begins by checking whether it is already selected and returns without doing anything if it is. A tap on the mode you are already in is therefore free. Tap vs Friend: if the stones stay where they are, the table was already in two-player mode. If they vanish, it was not, and you have bought that answer with one position instead of four minutes of argument. Give that tap to one person and to nobody else.

The grey rows read faster. In two-player mode the difficulty row and the colour row go dim and stop responding, so two grey rows is a complete answer to the question a host asks every ten minutes.

In two-player mode there is no such thing as out of turn

Against the computer, a tap while the AI is thinking does nothing at all. Two-player mode has no such insurance. One of the two colors is always on move, so every touch is a legal move, including the one from someone walking past the table. Time it badly and the stone lands on the opponent’s behalf.

Undo puts that right in one tap. Two-player undo removes exactly the last stone and hands the turn back to whoever placed it, so a stray touch costs a tap, not a game. The mirror of that is worth saying out loud: anyone at the table can delete the move you just made, one tap, no question. Undo belongs to the same person as the mode row.

What the scoreboard is counting, which is not this evening

Three counters: black wins, white wins, draws. They are set once when the page loads, never cleared between games, and they count colors rather than people. At 7:40 the tablet reads black 3, white 1. That is four games played by five different people, and none of those numbers knows who was black in any of them.

A reload zeroes it. The only trace the app keeps of the afternoon disappears in the same gesture that fixes a stuck tap. Paper, one sheet, three columns: game number, who took black, result. Nothing else survives the evening, and nothing else needs to.

The twenty-second reset, in order

Run it before the first stone, not after the first disagreement. The person about to take black runs it, because he is the only one at the table with both hands free and no move to lose.

  1. Look, do not tap. Three rows carry the whole state: mode, rule set, board size. The lit button in each row is the only place the setting is written down. Reading is free. Tapping can cost a game.
  2. Check for grey. Two dim rows means two-player mode. If the difficulty buttons are lit, somebody has put the computer back on the far side of the board.
  3. Start with Restart, never the browser reload. Restart clears the stones and keeps the settings. Reload clears the stones, the settings and the score.
  4. Say out loud who takes black. Every game here begins with black, and in two-player mode no button on the page changes that. The first move is not something the screen decides, it is something the table decides, once per game. Left unsaid, the same two people sit down every round and the fourth game becomes a rerun of the first.
  5. If the round is renju, one sentence: black cannot make six. The refusal holds for two and a half seconds, never says which rule was broken, and gives the turn back to black. Three refusals in a row is a guest concluding the tablet is faulty.
  6. Write the result before the next pair sits down. Ten seconds, on paper, or the evening ends with a scoreboard nobody can interpret.

The minutes assume a party pace of about ten seconds a move: twenty-odd moves on 9×9, forty-odd on 15×15, sixty-odd on 19×19. If the table is trying to play fast on purpose, the budget becomes a different exercise and belongs in its own piece. With two screens in the room, which one runs renju is decided by the two-table arithmetic, not by preference.

Three generations, one board, and the handicap the reset cannot set

There is no handicap setting. Stones cannot be spotted before the game, there is no pass, and there is no way to place two and hand over. The only stone a stronger player can give is a move spent on a dead point at the edge: first or last row, first or last file, and then kept out of every line he needs. On 9×9 that works twice. Three is not a handicap, it is a resignation.

The cheaper lever is the forced black. Hand black to the child, or to whoever has not played since 2019. On 9×9 the first move is worth more than any junk stone. On 15×15 the gap is wide enough that a beginner holding the first move can stay with a decent adult for twenty moves, which makes a good evening for both of them.

Then match the rule to the supervision. Freestyle wherever no adult is within earshot. Renju is the better game and the more interesting one to teach, but a two-and-a-half-second toast read from the kitchen is not a rule, it is a rumour. The order a child can actually hold the rules in is five in a row, then blocks, then fouls, and fouls come last because they are the only part that needs a second person to explain.

If the table is running a queue rather than pairs, the format decides black for you: the ladder hands the first move to the challenger every time, and the reset collapses to two steps, because the fourth one is already in the rules.

Keep your fingers off the reload

If you change one habit this year, change that one. Restart is a single tap and keeps everything the table agreed on. Reload is a coin flip on whether the next game is renju or freestyle, whether it is two people or a machine, and whether the afternoon’s score still exists. The reset takes twenty seconds and the argument it prevents takes about four minutes; across a ninety-minute evening that difference is one and a half games, which is more than any handicap you can hand out.
