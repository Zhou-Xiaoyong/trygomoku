King of the Hill at Thanksgiving: One Screen, One Queue, No Bracket

2026-11-08 8 min read

Six people, one tablet, and a bracket that is already broken by the third game: the cousin who took black won twice, nobody wrote down against whom, and the second round has no idea who advances.

There is a format that needs none of that. No draw, no sheet, no scorekeeper. One chair, one queue, a three-game cap. Same afternoon, run as a ladder instead of a bracket, and it survives contact with a holiday kitchen far better than anything you can print.

The queue holds one piece of information

A bracket holds a history: who played whom, who won, who is owed a rematch. A queue holds one fact, and it is on the screen already. Who is sitting in the chair right now.

That difference comes from a limit this board cannot hide. There is no online mode. The two-player setting puts both colors on one device and alternates taps, so exactly one game exists at a time, anywhere in the house. A bracket assumes games run in parallel. They do not. How two-player mode actually behaves covers the mechanics; tonight the useful part is the consequence. Six people cost six times one game, in series, and anything that has to be written down gets written down wrong.

Ladder arithmetic first, with numbers you can check. Two people who know the rules finish 15×15 in 40 to 45 moves, which is about 8 minutes at 12 seconds a move. The same pair finishes 9×9 in 20 to 24 moves, about 4 minutes. Ninety minutes of table time is therefore 11 games on the big board, or 20 on the small one.

Three numbers and the format is finished

Eight minutes a game. That is the 15×15 figure above, and it is what you promise the person standing behind the chair.

Twenty seconds a move. The board has no clock. No countdown, no increment, nothing in the interface that measures time, so the clock is the phone of whoever is waiting. Twenty seconds is generous for a strong player and tight for someone counting a line. Playing under a clock has the per-game version if your table would rather bank five minutes each.

Three games as king. Not for fairness. For the queue. Three games at eight minutes is 24 minutes, the longest anyone should stand there holding a plate. Win three and you walk to the back of the line like everyone else. That one rule does more for the mood of the room than any handicap you can invent.

The king is whoever is sitting down

First king: oldest person, host, or the kid. It does not matter, because from the second game the seat fills itself. Win and you stay. Lose and you walk to the end of the queue and the challenger sits. Nobody volunteers, nobody gets asked, nobody has to be talked out of the chair at eleven o'clock.

Say it out loud before the first stone, in one sentence: winner stays, loser goes to the back, three wins and you go to the back too.

Black moves first in every game, and no button changes that

Go looking for a color switch in two-player mode and you will not find one. The black and white buttons grey out the moment the second player joins, and the board refuses the change outright. Every new game opens with black to move.

So the first-move advantage cannot be rotated by tapping something. It rotates by agreement: this game I take white, and you tap first. Write that into the ladder and the problem disappears. The challenger always takes black. The king defends white, every time.

This is the one rule in the format that is not about logistics. Black in freestyle gomoku on a big board is not a small edge, it is a solved advantage, which is precisely why it belongs to the person climbing the hill. The king's streak becomes honest. If the eight-year-old holds the chair for three games while playing white, she earned it, and the four adults watching will know she earned it.

The screen scoreboard is useless for this, by the way. Black, white and draws are tallied per color, survive a new game, and vanish on a refresh, and they have no idea who was holding which color. Use paper. Two columns, a name and a tally, ten seconds.

Thanksgiving, three generations, one chair

This is where a ladder beats a bracket. In a bracket the eight-year-old loses in round one and watches television for the rest of the afternoon. In a ladder she is in the queue all night and gets three or four turns at the chair.

The handicap has one constraint a physical board does not have. There is no pass button and no way to place stones before the game starts. Stones go down alternately, one at a time. A handicap therefore has to be paid during play, by the stronger player throwing stones away: outer line only, and one empty point between them so two rim stones cannot quietly turn into a pair.

Two of those on 9×9 is a real spot. Three on 9×9 is a resignation with paperwork, and anyone who has watched a grandparent hand over three stones knows it. Cap it at two on the small board, three on the big one.

Shrink the board before you add stones. Nine lines turns a mismatch into a game faster than any handicap, because a small board rewards looking at the whole position instead of counting a long line. If the gap is large, play 9×9 with two rim stones and skip 15×15 entirely. The kids' rules walkthrough has the wording that works when somebody is learning the game at the same table.

One more thing about the child in the chair, because this is the part hosts get backwards. Cap her at three wins like everyone else, then make a show of it. A kid who wins six in a row and never gives up the seat ends the game for the four adults watching. A kid who wins three, gets applauded, and is told the seat is not hers forever will play again next year.

Three things that end a ladder early

Someone switches to the computer mid-game. Every mode button on this board resets the position when it is tapped, including the switch back. If a guest wants to see the AI play, finish the game first or open a second tab. Otherwise the queue watches thirty moves vanish because somebody wanted a demo.

Somebody presses Hint. It pulses the highest-rated empty point for three seconds, then fades. No explanation, no variation, no reason given. In a ladder that is not help, it is the king being handed the answer while the challenger watches. Agree before the first game that nobody presses it.

Renju stays on. The foul warning lasts two and a half seconds, in a room where six people are talking and an oven door is open. Worse, this board treats a foul as a refused move: the stone comes off, black plays again, the position is unchanged. Tournament renju loses the game on the spot for a sixth black stone. A guest who has been taught the real rule will think the tablet froze. Freestyle, fouls off, all night.

Five lines on a card

  1. Winner stays. Loser goes to the back. Three wins and you go to the back too.
  2. Challenger takes black. The king takes white.
  3. Nine lines for anyone under twelve or over seventy. Two rim stones from the stronger player, outer line, one point apart.
  4. Twenty seconds a move, timed by whoever is in the queue.
  5. No hint, no Renju, no switching modes until the game ends.

One card, taped to the table edge and facing the host, is the entire paperwork. If the room needs a second table of games, the wider plan for the evening sits in running a family game night; the ladder only settles how this one chair keeps moving.

Before the doorbell, do one thing. Open the board, set 9×9 and the two-player mode, and play one full game against yourself with a stopwatch running. Under five minutes and six people fit through the evening. Eight minutes and you drop the king cap to two and put the child in the chair first.
