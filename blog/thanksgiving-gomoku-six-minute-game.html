The Six-Minute Game: Thanksgiving Gomoku for the Hour After Dinner

2026-11-17 8 min read

Seven-twenty on Thanksgiving, plates stacked at one end of the table, and somebody says let's play. Twenty-five minutes later two people are still on move thirty-one and the other three have found their phones. Nobody was slow. The game was long.

Fifteen lines produce forty to forty-five moves. At twelve seconds a move that is nine minutes of two people's evening, and nine minutes of standing around for everyone else. The answer is not to think faster. It is to put the same skill into a smaller box.

Nothing on this page runs a clock

No countdown, no increment, no nudge when a player takes too long. The only thing that controls how long a game lasts is how many moves it needs, and that is settled the second the board size changes: twenty to twenty-four moves on 9×9, forty to forty-five on 15×15, sixty-five to seventy on 19×19.

So short is not a pace, it is a board. Nine lines is the short format, and it does not feel like a learner's board. It feels like a board where every stone is carrying weight. That comes out of the geometry rather than anybody's taste, and it is the same reason a twenty-minute game holds up better than a long one on a weeknight.

Nine lines is a denser game, not a smaller one

On a line of nine points there are five places a five can begin, and all five of them span the middle point. Any five you make on that line goes through it. A stone on the e-file or the e-row therefore sits inside every winning line that line will ever produce, which is why the center point fills on move one and stays argued over until the end.

Fifteen lines have eleven starting places and exactly one of them crosses the middle point. There the center is a slow positional argument. On 9×9 it is the whole argument.

Two things follow, and both are usable tonight. Games on 9×9 are usually decided between move six and move eight, because by then each player has a pair and the third stone of either pair is already a threat. And the four corner star points are not decoration: c3, c7, g3 and g7 are each the midpoint of two five-point diagonals, so a stone there already sits in the middle of two lines you may need later.

Where the six minutes go

Twelve moves each, and the arithmetic is simple. Three seconds for the opening stone, twelve seconds for each of the next three, twenty seconds for the six moves in the middle, which is the only expensive stretch, eight seconds for each of the last two. That is a bit over a hundred and seventy seconds per player. Under six minutes for the two of them.

The arithmetic has a use. The whole cost sits in those six middle moves. Hurrying the ending is pointless, the ending is seconds by nature. There are only two ways to spend less: shorten the middle, meaning build a threat earlier so the opponent has one or two answers instead of four, or agree that the game does not count and stops at move twenty.

Renju costs two and a half seconds every time it refuses a move

Eighty-one points fill quickly, and black's forbidden shapes arrive early. The density that produces a three-three or a double-four peaks somewhere around move nine to twelve, which is the middle of a short game rather than the end of a long one.

A forbidden point is not a loss on this board. The stone comes back off the grid, a warning sits in the status bar for two and a half seconds, and the turn is still black's, so the price is the wait plus a plan the player has to rebuild. Three refusals in one game is seven seconds of nothing and three rebuilt plans. In an eleven-minute game that is absorbable. In a six-minute game it is a tenth of the game gone. Use Freestyle for the short format and keep renju for the two adults on 15×15 who want the restriction that makes the opening fair.

The hint button is the only control that ends a game

Hint does one thing. It scores the empty points for whoever is on move, pulses the best one for three seconds, then forgets it. No reason, no line of play, no follow-up. In a forty-move game that is a small cheat. In a twenty-move game it is the answer, because on nine lines the top-scored point is usually the winning point and there are only a few moves left in which to spend it.

It is also live in two-player mode, and it always answers for whoever is on move, so the person about to place a stone can call it up while the other watches. Decide before the first stone: nobody presses it. If it gets pressed, the game is over and the person who pressed it loses. Everyone remembers that rule and nobody wants to test it.

Short games survive interruptions, which is the real argument

A tablet that sleeps and wakes to a reloaded page has no game on it. No save, no account, no stored position: the page comes back at 15×15, Freestyle, vs Computer, Medium. Whatever was on the board is gone with no trace and no score.

The exposure is proportional to the length of the game, and this is the part that gets skipped. A six-minute game that dies at move twelve costs six minutes. A twelve-minute game that dies at move thirty-five costs twelve, and it costs them after the table has already invested in the result. Short games are not only faster. They are the format whose interruptions are cheap enough to shrug at.

What the short format cannot do

It teaches almost nothing. On a dense board the losing move is three moves back, the grid carries no move numbers, and the only marks at the end are the line through the five and the ring on the last stone. There is no history panel to walk backwards through, so the position cannot be rebuilt at the table.

So do not put a guest who wants to learn into it. Give that person 15×15 vs Computer on Easy, where the levels genuinely behave differently, and let them lose five games without an audience. Six minutes is for people who know the rules and want five games before the coffee cools.

The short game is already the handicap

Nobody has to spot stones to a grandparent here, and that is the quiet advantage of twenty moves. Skill compounds over a long game: forty-four moves give the stronger player forty-four chances to turn a small edge into a bigger one. Twenty moves give him ten, and two of those get spent answering a threat instead of building one.

So the pairing that works at Thanksgiving is nine lines and five games, not fifteen lines and a lecture about handicap stones. Over five short games the results spread out, which is what a holiday table wants: nobody goes 5-0, nobody goes 0-5, and the eight-year-old leaves with a story. If you want one more lever, give the child Black. The first move is the only concession this board can make, and on nine lines it is a real one.

What board size will not fix is the adult who last played in college. He loses to the child not because the child calculates deeper but because the child has seen a hundred open threes this year and he has seen four. Tell him one rule before the first stone: a three with both ends open has to be answered now, everything else can wait a move. That is worth more than any handicap you can arrange here.

The fourth column is the one that saves the evening. A cap agreed before the first stone stops the player who is winning from turning one stone into a four-minute explanation while three people wait. When the cap arrives, stop: restart the board, and accept that the counter will not record it, because an abandoned position is invisible to a scoreboard that only counts finished games.

Two short games beat one long one for a reason that has nothing to do with skill. The second game is where the loser gets to try the idea he did not get to try. Running rounds between dinner and pie is the same arithmetic read from the other end.

Five things on a card

Nine lines. Freestyle. Call it at twenty. Nobody presses hint. Hand the tablet to whoever lost.

That is the whole format, and it fits on an index card next to the salt. The board is one click away, the board-size button takes a second, and the first five is about six minutes out.
