Gomoku Timed Mode: How to Play Fast Without Handing Over the Board

2026-11-03 8 min read

Move Thirty-Four, Nine Seconds Left, and a Sixth Stone in the Row

Black has h4 through h8. h9 is empty, the phone stopwatch says nine seconds, and he plays h9 without counting what is already on the line.

That is six stones in a row. In renju black loses immediately: an overline, six or more where the rules demand exactly five. In freestyle the same stone wins.

Nothing about the position was difficult. Counting the row took two seconds. The clock took them first. A timed game is not the same game played faster, because the moves that vanish under pressure are not the clever ones. They are the checks.

There Is No Clock Here, So Bring One

The controls are difficulty (Easy, Medium, Hard, Master), board size (9, 15, 19), rule set (freestyle or renju) and colour. No timer, no increment, no per-move countdown. You can think for four minutes on move six if you want to.

So timed gomoku means a convention you impose yourself. Three of them work.

  • Ten seconds a stone. Good for drilling, useless for serious planning. You learn to see instead of calculate, which is what most players actually need.
  • Five minutes a side, whole game. The blitz shape. A 44-stone game leaves each side 22 stones in 300 seconds: 13.6 seconds a stone on average. One forty-second think costs three stones of clock.
  • Twenty stones in two minutes. Count moves, not seconds. Useful when two people keep drifting into a slow positional argument.

Use per-move caps for practice and total time for matches. Mixing them is how you flag on move 30 with a winning position on the board.

Three Moves Deserve the Clock

  1. The first four he makes that could start a VCF. A continuous-four chain from a single four runs eight to fourteen stones. Either you check it now or you never get another turn in which to check it.
  2. The stone where you commit. The move that turns a quiet position into a forcing one. Everything before it is reversible. This one is not.
  3. Any point that might be forbidden for you. Black, renju, and you are about to place. Fifteen seconds, every time.

Everything else gets three seconds: the block you already chose, the second four of a chain you have read, the recapture. Extra time on those buys nothing, and it is time you will want on move 34.

Quiet Moves Cost Him Time, Forcing Moves Do Not

A four has one legal answer. Checking it costs you two seconds because there is nothing to weigh. A quiet stone with five plausible continuations costs whoever faces it a minute, since all five have to be priced.

That asymmetry decides how to spend a clock, and it runs in both directions.

  • Your clock is low, his is fine. Play forcing moves. You verify a four in two seconds and the decision goes back to him.
  • His clock is low, yours is fine. Play quiet stones that do two things at once, three rows apart. He cannot answer both cheaply, and the side he answers badly is the side you attack.

Under pressure most players do the opposite. They rush the forcing move because it feels decisive, when the quiet two-purpose stone is what actually costs the opponent time. In a 5+5 game the second stone of a double threat is worth more than the four you were proud of.

Fast Play and Forbidden Points Do Not Mix

Black loses three ways in renju beyond being outplayed: overline, double four, double three. Each needs a different set of lines counted through the same point. Doing all three by hand takes about fifteen seconds, which is more than a ten-second cap allows.

Two honest fixes. Play freestyle when the cap is under ten seconds, because freestyle has no forbidden points and six in a row simply wins. Or keep renju and check one thing only: the row you are extending, counted in both directions. The overline is the foul that ends games, and it arrives while you are winning, which is exactly when nobody looks.

The double three is the quieter trap. A point that makes two open threes looks like the strongest stone on the board and loses on the spot for black. If you cannot afford to check it, play the single three.

Bank Time Before Move Eight

Black opens at the centre, h8. White's second stone either touches that stone (direct opening, chokushi) or sits one intersection away (indirect, kansetsu). Those two families generate the twenty-six named renju openings.

Kagetsu and Ugetsu are the two black players learn first, because renju theory grades both as wins for black. Knowing one of them to the eighth stone takes an evening and buys a full minute of clock for the middle game. Improvising on move three costs that minute in every single game.

Board size is the other lever. A 9x9 game runs about 24 stones in total, so five minutes a side is comfortable and three is playable. The same clock on 15x15 is a scramble. With a beginner, shrink the board rather than tightening the clock.

The Last Thirty Seconds

  1. Count his fours. One unblocked four ends the game in two stones. Your live three needs three more, and his four arrives first.
  2. Look for the block that also builds. His live three at d7, e7, f7 leaves two closing points, c7 and g7. If your c file already holds c4, c5 and c6, then c7 blocks him and makes your own open four at the same time.
  3. Stop planning two-move wins. At ten seconds you can verify a four and the single reply to it. Anything past that is imagination.
  4. If nothing is forcing, play the middle of the largest empty area. The stone is hard to answer quickly, and it keeps every line through it short.

A Drill Worth Twenty Games

Medium, ten seconds a stone, stopwatch on the table. After each game write the cause of the loss in one word: foul, block, clock.

Most players expect clock to dominate. It usually does not. Foul and block come first, and both are checks rather than calculations. That is the whole lesson of fast gomoku: you do not get worse at thinking, you stop verifying.

Then play the same twenty at five minutes a side. If the results barely move, time was never the problem and those hours belong to reading depth instead.

One thing to know before you start: the AI answers on its own budget, not yours. Master keeps deepening until twelve seconds pass or the position goes quiet, so a fast game does not make you the quicker player at that table. Playing the computer on its terms is a separate problem, and the level-by-level breakdown covers what each tier actually values.

Open a board, set Medium, put the stopwatch where you can see it. The first game feels frantic. By the fifth, ten seconds feel like enough, and that is the only result worth chasing.
